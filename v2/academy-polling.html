The Simple Version

A poll is an attempt to measure what a group thinks

Pollsters ask questions to a group of people and use those responses to describe public opinion.

The quality of a poll depends on how people were selected, how the questions were asked, how many people responded, and how the results were analyzed.

A poll is not automatically a perfect picture of what every person thinks.

Basics How Does a Poll Work?
+

Polls ask a sample of people questions

A poll usually does not ask every person in the population.

Instead, pollsters collect responses from a smaller group called a sample.

They then use that sample to estimate what a larger population may think.

Who Was Asked? What Is a Sample?
+

A sample is the group of people who took the poll

The sample matters because the people who respond may not perfectly match the larger population.

A stronger poll tries to build a sample that reflects the population it is trying to measure.

Representation What Is a Representative Sample?
+

A representative sample tries to reflect the larger group

If a poll is meant to describe American adults, the sample should not come almost entirely from one age group, region, or political group.

Pollsters may use sampling methods and weighting to make the final results better reflect the population they are studying.

Important

A large sample is not automatically representative.

Who is included can matter as much as how many people participate.

Size Does Sample Size Matter?
+

Yes, but bigger is not the only thing that matters

Larger samples can reduce some forms of sampling uncertainty.

But a very large sample can still produce misleading results if the people participating do not reflect the population being studied.

Simple example

Asking 100,000 people from only one political group would not automatically tell you what all Americans think.

Uncertainty What Is Margin of Error?
+

Margin of error shows a range of sampling uncertainty

In many probability-based polls, the margin of error gives an estimate of how much the reported result could differ from the result we might get if the entire population were surveyed.

A smaller margin of error generally means less sampling uncertainty.

Simple example

If a poll reports 52% support with a margin of error of plus or minus 3 points, the underlying population result could reasonably be several points higher or lower.

Important

Margin of error does not capture every possible source of polling error.

Question wording, nonresponse, sampling problems, weighting, and other factors can also affect results.

Survey Design Does Question Wording Matter?
+

Yes. The words used in a question can affect responses

Poll questions should be written as clearly and neutrally as possible.

Leading, emotionally loaded, confusing, or one-sided wording can influence how people answer.

Simple example

“Do you support the proposal?” and “Do you support the costly proposal that critics say will raise taxes?” are not asking the question in the same way.

What to look for

When reading a poll, check the exact wording of the question whenever possible.

Responses What About Undecided or Unsure Responses?
+

Not everyone has an opinion on every question

Some respondents may be undecided, unsure, or unwilling to choose one of the offered answers.

Poll results can look different depending on whether those responses are shown separately, excluded, or redistributed in an analysis.

Transparency matters

A poll should make clear how undecided, unsure, or missing responses were handled.

Comparison Why Can Different Polls Get Different Results?
+

Different polls can use different methods

Two polls asking about the same issue can produce different results because they may differ in who was surveyed, how respondents were selected, when the poll was conducted, and how questions were worded.

Different sample
Different dates
Different question wording
Different weighting
Different likely-voter models
Normal sampling variation
One poll is a snapshot

Looking at several credible polls over time can sometimes provide more context than relying on a single result.

Geography National Polls vs. State Polls
+

They measure different populations

A national poll tries to measure opinion across the country.

A state poll focuses on people in one state.

The result of a national poll does not automatically tell you what voters in a particular state think.

Why this matters in presidential elections

Presidents are chosen through the Electoral College, so state-level opinion can matter differently from the national popular vote.

Measurement Polls vs. Elections
+

A poll is not an election result

Polls measure opinions or voting intentions at a particular point in time.

Elections count actual ballots cast according to election law.

Simple example

A candidate leading a poll does not mean that candidate has already won the election.

Opinion can change

People can change their minds between the date of a poll and Election Day.

Reading Results How Should You Read a Poll?
+

Look beyond the headline number

A useful poll should provide enough information for people to understand how the result was produced.

Who conducted the poll?
Who was surveyed?
How many people participated?
When was the poll conducted?
How were respondents selected?
What exact question was asked?
Was weighting used?
What uncertainty is reported?
The headline is only the beginning

Methodology and question wording can be just as important as the final percentage.

Warning Signs What Can Make a Poll Misleading?
+

Some poll claims deserve extra scrutiny

No sample information
No dates provided
No exact question wording
Strongly leading questions
Unclear population
Results presented without context
Tiny subgroup results
Claiming one poll proves what everyone thinks
Transparency is the test

If you cannot tell who was asked, what was asked, or how the result was calculated, you should be cautious about treating the result as representative.

Civic Horizon Results What Do Our Poll Results Mean?
+

Our results show how participating Civic Horizon users responded

When Civic Horizon publishes the results of a participant poll, the percentages describe the responses collected through Civic Horizon.

Unless a poll uses a methodology that supports broader population estimates, those results should not automatically be described as representing every American.

Example

If 62% of Civic Horizon participants choose an answer, the accurate statement is:

“62% of Civic Horizon participants who voted in this poll selected this answer.”

That is different from saying:

“62% of Americans believe this.”

Participation Why Should We Show the Number of Participants?
+

Percentages need context

A percentage by itself does not tell you how many people participated.

Civic Horizon should display the number of valid responses alongside poll results whenever practical.

Example

70% based on 100 participants and 70% based on 100,000 participants are the same percentage, but the participation counts are very different.

Context Why Do Participant Demographics Matter?
+

Who participates can affect the results

A participant group may differ from the broader population in age, geography, political affiliation, or other characteristics.

When Civic Horizon has enough reliable data to provide useful demographic context, that information can help participants understand who contributed to a result.

Privacy still matters

Transparency about poll participation should not require exposing an individual participant's private voting choices.

Transparency Should Civic Horizon Show the Exact Question?
+

Yes.

Participants should be able to see the exact question and answer choices used to produce a result.

This makes it easier to evaluate whether the wording was clear and neutral.

Important Distinction Is a Civic Horizon Vote a Scientific National Poll?
+

Not automatically.

A large number of responses does not by itself make a poll scientifically representative of the United States.

If people choose for themselves whether to participate, the resulting participant group can differ from the larger population.

This does not make the results useless

Civic Horizon participant results can still provide useful information about what the site's participating community thinks.

The key is describing those results accurately.

Research Standards Why Should Sources Be Visible?
+

Participants should be able to inspect the basis of a claim

Polling organizations often publish methodology statements, questionnaires, sample information, weighting procedures, and other technical details.

When those materials are available, they help readers evaluate the quality and meaning of the results.

The Big Picture

A percentage is only useful when you know how it was produced.

Good polling requires more than collecting answers. It requires a clear population, sound sampling, neutral questions, transparent methodology, and careful interpretation.

When reading any poll, ask: who was surveyed, what was asked, how the sample was created, and what the result actually represents.
