The Simple Version

Government collects money and spends money

Governments collect money mainly through taxes and other revenue.

That money is used to pay for public programs, services, employees, infrastructure, defense, benefits, and many other government responsibilities.

If the federal government spends more in a year than it collects, it runs a deficit.

Taxes What Are Taxes?
+

Taxes are required payments to government

Federal, state, and local governments collect different kinds of taxes.

Those taxes help pay for government programs, services, and responsibilities.

Common examples

Depending on where you live and your situation, you may encounter income taxes, payroll taxes, sales taxes, property taxes, or other taxes.

Types of Spending Mandatory vs. Discretionary Spending
+

Federal spending is commonly divided into categories

Mandatory spending is generally controlled by laws that set eligibility rules or payment formulas.

Discretionary spending is generally decided through the annual appropriations process.

Mandatory: Medicare

Simple way to think about it

Mandatory spending follows laws already in place. Discretionary spending is decided through annual funding decisions.

Budget What Is the Federal Budget?
+

The federal budget is a plan for government money

The federal budget shows expected revenue and proposed or authorized spending.

Congress plays the central role in deciding federal spending and taxes, while the President proposes a budget and signs or vetoes legislation.

Simple example

A federal budget may estimate how much money the government expects to collect and how much it expects to spend during a fiscal year.

Deficit What Is a Federal Budget Deficit?
+

A deficit happens when spending is greater than revenue

If the federal government spends more money during a fiscal year than it collects in revenue, the difference is called a budget deficit.

The government generally finances that difference by borrowing.

Do not confuse deficit with debt

A deficit is the shortfall during a period of time. The national debt is the accumulated amount the federal government owes from past borrowing.

Surplus What Is a Federal Budget Surplus?
+

A surplus is the opposite of a deficit

A budget surplus occurs when federal revenue is greater than federal spending during a fiscal year.

Simple comparison

Revenue greater than spending = surplus.

Spending greater than revenue = deficit.

Debt What Is the National Debt?
+

The national debt is the total amount the federal government owes

When the federal government runs deficits, it borrows money to cover the difference.

Over time, that borrowing adds to the total federal debt.

Simple way to think about it

Deficit = the shortfall during a period.

Debt = the accumulated amount owed from past borrowing.

Interest What Is Interest on the Debt?
+

Borrowing money has a cost

When the federal government borrows, it pays interest to the people and institutions that hold federal debt.

Interest payments are part of federal spending.

Why it matters

As debt and interest rates change, the amount the government spends on interest can also change.

Borrowing Limit What Is the Debt Ceiling?
+

The debt ceiling limits federal borrowing authority

The debt ceiling is a legal limit on the amount of debt the U.S. Treasury is authorized to issue.

It does not, by itself, create new spending. Instead, it affects the government's ability to borrow to meet obligations that have already been authorized.

Important distinction

Budget decisions determine spending and revenue.

The debt ceiling concerns the government's legal authority to borrow.

Federal Debt Who Owns the National Debt?
+

Federal debt is held by many different investors

U.S. Treasury securities are held by individuals, financial institutions, retirement funds, businesses, foreign investors, and government accounts.

The national debt is not owed to just one country, company, or group.

The Big Picture

Revenue, spending, deficit, and debt are different things.

Revenue is money the federal government collects. Spending is money the government pays out.

When spending exceeds revenue during a fiscal year, there is a deficit. Repeated borrowing contributes to federal debt.

Understanding those differences makes government budget discussions much easier to evaluate.
