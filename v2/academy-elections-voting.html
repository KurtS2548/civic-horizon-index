The Simple Version

There is no single national election system

Elections in the United States are highly decentralized.

States establish many of the rules and procedures for elections, while federal law establishes requirements that states must follow.

Elections are usually administered by local election officials, often at the county level, although the exact system varies by state.

Eligibility Who Can Vote?
+

Who can vote in federal elections?

To vote in a federal election, a person must be a U.S. citizen and meet the other eligibility requirements that apply in their state.

U.S. citizen
Meet state residency requirements
Age 18 by Election Day
Meet applicable registration requirements
Rules can vary

Registration deadlines and some other eligibility rules differ by state.

North Dakota is the only state that does not require voter registration.

Before Voting Voter Registration
+

Why do people register to vote?

Voter registration allows election officials to maintain a list of people who are legally registered to vote.

Registration procedures and deadlines vary by state.

Important

North Dakota does not require voters to register before voting.

Election Administration Who Actually Runs Elections?
+

Is there one federal office running every election?

No. American election administration is highly decentralized.

Each state has a chief election official, but elections are usually administered locally.

In many states, counties run elections. In some states, cities or townships perform that role.

A useful fact

The United States has more than 10,000 election jurisdictions.

Choosing Candidates Primary Elections
+

What is a primary election?

A primary election helps political parties choose which candidates will represent them in a later general election.

Primary rules are not the same everywhere. States and political parties can use different systems.

Simple way to think about it

A primary usually helps decide who will be on the ballot for a political party in the general election.

Choosing Officeholders General Elections
+

What is a general election?

A general election is the election in which voters choose among the candidates running for public office.

General-election ballots can include candidates for federal, state, county, and local offices, depending on the election.

Simple example

A November ballot might include candidates for President, Congress, governor, state legislature, county offices, local offices, judges, or ballot questions.

Many decisions that affect daily life — schools, roads, zoning, public safety, and local taxes — can be influenced by state and local elections.

After Voting How Are Votes Counted?
+

What happens after ballots are cast?

Election officials count ballots according to the laws and procedures that apply in their state and local jurisdiction.

The process can include checking ballot records, counting in-person and mail ballots, reviewing certain ballots that need additional verification, and officially certifying results.

Election Night is not always the final result

News organizations may project winners, but official election results come from election authorities after required counting, review, and certification procedures are completed.

Why can counting take time?

Different states have different rules for processing mail ballots, provisional ballots, recounts, and certification.

President How Is the President Elected?
+

The President is chosen through the Electoral College

When people vote for President, their votes help determine which presidential electors represent their state.

Those electors later cast the official electoral votes for President and Vice President.

Important

The President is not elected directly by a nationwide popular vote.

Presidential Elections What Is the Electoral College?
+

It is a process, not a place

The Electoral College is the constitutional process used to elect the President and Vice President.

It includes choosing electors, the electors casting their votes, and Congress counting those electoral votes.

538 total electors
270 electoral votes needed to win
Each state receives electors based on its representation in Congress
Washington, D.C. receives 3 electors
Electoral Votes How Does Your State Award Its Electoral Votes?
+

Most states use a winner-take-all system

In 48 states and Washington, D.C., the presidential candidate who wins the statewide popular vote receives all of that state's electoral votes.

Maine and Nebraska are different

Maine and Nebraska award two electors based on the statewide vote and one elector based on the winner in each congressional district.

Popular Vote Can Someone Win the Popular Vote but Lose the Election?
+

Yes.

The nationwide popular vote and the Electoral College are two different totals.

A candidate can receive more votes nationwide but still receive fewer electoral votes.

This has happened multiple times in U.S. history, including the presidential elections of 2000 and 2016.

Unusual Situation What If Nobody Gets 270 Electoral Votes?
+

Congress decides under constitutional rules

If no presidential candidate receives a majority of electoral votes, the House of Representatives chooses the President under the process established by the Constitution.

The Senate chooses the Vice President if no vice-presidential candidate receives an electoral-vote majority.

Law & Evidence

Noncitizen Voting

This section separates what the law says, documented cases, available research, and what the available data cannot tell us.

Federal Law Can Noncitizens Vote in Federal Elections?
+

No.

U.S. citizenship is required to vote in federal elections.

This includes lawful permanent residents and other people living in the United States who have not become U.S. citizens.

Local elections can be different

Some jurisdictions allow noncitizens to vote in certain local elections.

That does not give a noncitizen the right to vote in a federal election.

Terminology Does “Noncitizen” Mean “Undocumented”?
+

No. They are not the same category.

“Noncitizen” means a person who is not a citizen of the United States.

That broad category can include lawful permanent residents and other people who are legally present, as well as people who are unlawfully present.

Why this distinction matters

When discussing noncitizen voting, evidence about all noncitizens should not automatically be described as evidence specifically about undocumented immigrants.

Documented Cases Has Illegal Noncitizen Voting Happened?
+

Yes.

There are documented cases in which noncitizens have illegally registered, voted in federal elections, or both.

Federal prosecutors have brought cases involving noncitizens who illegally voted, including cases resulting in guilty pleas and convictions.

Documented example

In June 2026, the U.S. Department of Justice announced that three noncitizens had pleaded guilty in separate federal cases involving illegal voting and related election offenses.

Facts require precision

A criminal charge or indictment is an allegation unless and until guilt is established. A guilty plea or conviction establishes a different level of evidence.

Available Research How Often Does It Happen?
+

There is no reliable all-time national percentage.

The United States does not have a complete national historical database identifying every instance in which a noncitizen illegally cast a ballot.

Because of that, an exact percentage covering every U.S. election throughout history cannot be responsibly calculated from available data.

One Large 2016 Study About 30 suspected incidents

Researchers surveyed election officials representing 42 jurisdictions that together oversaw approximately 23.5 million votes in the 2016 general election.

Officials reported approximately 30 incidents of suspected noncitizen voting that were referred for further investigation or prosecution.

Approximately 0.0001%

That was approximately 0.0001% of votes cast in the jurisdictions included in that study.

What that number does NOT mean

It is not an all-time U.S. noncitizen voting rate.

It does not cover every jurisdiction in the country.

The approximately 30 incidents were suspected cases referred for investigation or prosecution, not 30 automatically proven illegal votes.

Election Integrity

Election Security & Safeguards

Election systems use multiple procedures to help verify voters, protect ballots, identify problems, and confirm results.

Protection What Safeguards Are Used?
+

Election security uses multiple layers

The exact procedures differ by state, but election officials use a variety of safeguards throughout the election process.

Voter registration records
Voter eligibility checks
Ballot tracking and accounting
Voting-system testing
Physical security procedures
Chain-of-custody procedures
Post-election audits
Certification procedures
Important

The exact safeguards and procedures are not identical in every state.

Verification Are There Paper Records?
+

Paper records are widely used

Most votes in U.S. elections are cast using systems that produce or use a paper record.

Paper records can provide election officials with a physical record that can be examined during audits or recounts.

Why this matters

A paper record can provide an independent way to check electronically reported totals.

Verification What Is a Post-Election Audit?
+

An audit checks election results

Post-election audits can compare ballots or paper records with reported vote totals to help verify that voting systems produced accurate results.

Audit requirements and methods vary from state to state.

Close Elections What Is a Recount?
+

A recount counts the votes again

A recount is a process for reviewing or counting ballots again after an election.

State laws determine when recounts occur, who may request one, and how the recount is conducted.

Audit and recount are not the same thing

An audit is generally a verification procedure. A recount involves counting votes again under applicable election law.

Accuracy Can Election Errors or Illegal Activity Occur?
+

Yes.

Election administration involves people, technology, records, and procedures. Errors can occur, and election crimes can occur.

Election laws and safeguards exist in part to prevent problems, identify them when they occur, correct errors when possible, and investigate potential violations.

Evidence matters

The existence of an error or illegal act does not by itself establish how widespread a problem is or whether it changed an election result.

Those are separate factual questions that require evidence.
